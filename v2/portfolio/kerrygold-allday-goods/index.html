KerrygoldThe Allday Goods Butter Knife Drop

We treated a butter knife launch like a sneaker drop. The result? Queues at 6am. Two sold out cities. A collaboration that took the internet by storm.

Trust doesn't make people queue up and anticipate product drops, but desire does. Kerrygold had spent decades building the trust, and it had never seriously gone after the desire. Across markets, everyone already believed the brand was great. Almost nobody had a reason to want it the way people craved a limited edition sneaker drop or a sold-out pop-up restaurant booking. That gap, between being trusted and being desired, was the one I wanted to close for the brand.

The idea we landed on internally was to stop thinking like a food brand and start thinking like a culture focused streetwear label - which is an area of marketing, brand and culture I've had incredible respect and an interest in for years. So, why not partner with a local maker people already queue for? I wanted to build something genuinely limited and special. Let the object do the work a media budget usually has to. That's when we stumbled upon Allday Goods, a London maker known for hand-forged knives with handles built from recycled plastic - these guys made sense precisely because they had nothing to do with the FMCG marketing space at the time. The collaboration brief was simple: a limited batch of fifty knives, each numbered, blade hand-forged in Japan, handle made from repurposed Kerrygold packaging, swirled gold and green, no two ever the same.

I built the entire go-to-market around that idea. Teaser content in Allday Goods' own illustration style, so it never looked like Kerrygold had bolted its logo onto someone else's craft, produced with the incredible team at 1000Heads in London. A one-day pop-up at East London's De Beauvoir Deli. A second activation in Dublin's Temple Bar, so the story landed in the home market too, not just in London. I ran the creator outreach and PR, getting knives to people who'd genuinely care about them, and made a point of not stopping at the big names. I pulled together a list of Kerrygold's most loyal fans across social, people who'd been creating UGC and talking about the brand consistently long before any of this existed, and sent knives to them too.

All special edition knives sold out

It worked past the point of a good launch. People queued from 6am in the cold outside a deli in East London for a butter knife. James Kavanagh, one of Ireland's most loved foodie creators, unboxing TikTok pulled over 250,000 views within a few days. @whatwillycook's chaotic take added a second wave of attention for the UK market we didn't anticipate. The knives sold out twice, and kept selling out afterward, Allday Goods have restocked multiple times since, and as of early 2026 it's still one of the things people point to when they talk about Kerrygold looking genuinely desirable, and not just reliable.

A butter brand isn't really supposed to be the thing people set an alarm for, travel halfway across the country and queue up at 6am for in the freezing Winter months. For one morning in North London, and again in Dublin, it was.
